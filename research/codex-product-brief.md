# Codex product brief

> **Historical research, 2 November 2026.** A prose description of the intended
> product, written in a Codex session. It's folded into the
> [vision](../handbook/product/01-vision.md) and the
> [capability map](../handbook/product/02-capabilities.md), which are current.

DuraPOS is a free, open-source point-of-sale and daily business record system for Zimbabwean vendors, market traders, tuckshops and small shops. Its purpose is to help a business record what it sells, understand where its money goes, manage stock and outstanding balances, and keep dependable records that the owner can access independently. The central idea is business control: the owner controls the records, payment accounts and exports, and can continue using those records or migrate to another system without depending on Dura to release them.

This describes the intended product. The current application has its engineering foundation; most trading, financial, synchronisation and account-management functionality remains planned. Council-levy management is included here as a planned addition.

DuraPOS is designed around ordinary trading conditions in Zimbabwe: an affordable Android phone, intermittent internet, limited mobile data, power interruptions, cash transactions, multiple currencies, shortages of change, informal customer credit and small expenses paid directly from the day’s takings. The primary customer might be one person buying produce in the morning and selling throughout the day, an owner running a tuckshop with a relative, or a small shop employing a few cashiers. The intended scale is one phone through to a modest number of selling devices and locations. It should remain useful to the smallest business without requiring enterprise administration.

The core POS is intended to remain free to use without a mandatory Dura subscription. Businesses may choose paid assistance for setup, training, support, customisation or specialist integrations. They will still incur their own applicable costs, such as mobile data, payment-provider charges, printing or external services. Dura does not promise that those services become free. Its value is a dependable product and portable records, with optional assistance available when needed.

Starting a basic business should require only the information necessary to begin recording trade. The owner establishes the business, chooses its pricing currency, enters a few products or services and can begin recording appropriate cash sales. Importing an entire catalogue, connecting Google, enrolling staff, configuring payment gateways and enabling fiscalisation are separate capabilities that can be completed as needed. The target is basic onboarding in five minutes or less under ordinary conditions, without claiming that account recovery, merchant approval or regulatory registration can always fit into that time. A business with existing records should be able to import supported spreadsheets and opening balances after checking their meaning and accuracy.

The everyday selling function records products or services, quantities, agreed prices, discounts, payments and change. A sale can involve an anonymous walk-in customer; creating a customer account should only be necessary when the transaction needs an ongoing relationship, such as credit or change owed. Product names and search support ordinary selling, with barcode scanning available where useful. A business can record a service without maintaining a stock quantity for it. Completed sales retain the prices, quantities, currency information and other facts that applied when they occurred, so later catalogue changes do not alter previous transactions.

DuraPOS should support held or unfinished sales, cancelled drafts, authorised discounts, returns and refunds. These actions need clear financial meaning and a traceable relationship to the original transaction. A return may put usable goods back into stock, while damaged or spoiled goods may need a different stock treatment. A refund records money returned through the relevant payment method. Completed financial history is corrected through recorded reversals or adjustments, preserving the explanation and the person responsible.

Multiple currencies are part of the intended functionality, including USD, ZWG and ZAR where the business accepts them. The owner chooses the pricing currency; the dollar symbol in the logo does not establish a USD default. Dura records the currency and amount actually received, any conversion used, the rate applicable to the transaction, and the currency and amount of change given. It should support split payments where appropriate. Changes to an exchange rate apply prospectively and preserve previous transaction rates. Cash balances remain meaningful per currency, and consolidated reports require an explicit conversion basis rather than adding different currencies together.

Shortages of change deserve specific treatment. With the customer’s agreement, the business can record a small amount of change that it owes that customer. That amount remains a liability of the shop, with its original currency and a history of when it was created, redeemed or paid out. It can later be applied to a purchase or returned under the owner’s permissions. This is a record of money owed, with appropriate limits and controls. Where several devices operate offline, shared customer balances require restrictions or coordination to prevent the same balance being spent twice.

Customer credit is another optional capability. An owner can authorise particular customers to buy on account, establish limits and payment terms, and record the resulting debt. Invoices can remain unpaid, partly paid or fully paid, and later collections are linked to the original obligation. Receiving payment for an earlier invoice increases money received without creating a second sale. Customer statements should explain the transactions, payments and adjustments behind the outstanding amount. Quotes are also planned: they record a proposed supply and price without counting as completed revenue or reducing stock until converted into a sale.

Inventory functionality should explain what the business has and why quantities changed. It covers opening stock, goods received, goods sold, customer returns, supplier returns, transfers, stock counts, losses, spoilage and authorised adjustments. Products can use suitable units, including pieces, packs or measured quantities where supported. Optional stock control lets a service provider or very small trader use the sales functions without maintaining a detailed inventory. Low-stock information and purchasing suggestions should be grounded in recorded quantities and sales. Stock from another disconnected device may be out of date; Dura cannot guarantee a perfectly current shared quantity while devices are offline.

Suppliers and purchases complete that stock picture. A business can record who supplied goods, the supplier’s document reference, quantities, costs, payment terms and supporting invoice or receipt. Purchases can be paid from the till, paid by the owner, paid through another business account or left outstanding. Payments made later reduce the supplier balance and link back to the purchase. Buying stock, paying a supplier and recognising the cost of goods sold are related events with different meanings. Dura should preserve those differences so that buying tomorrow’s stock does not automatically make today’s trading performance appear worse.

Dura also records operating money movements that happen outside checkout. These include transport, rent, electricity, water, airtime, data, packaging, repairs, wages paid, bank charges, wallet charges and market-related costs. Owner contributions and owner drawings are recorded separately, allowing the owner to distinguish business performance from money introduced into or taken out of the business. Transfers between the business’s own cash, wallet and bank accounts preserve their identity as transfers. Recording a wage payment is useful, but a complete payroll system with statutory calculations is outside the initial scope.

Council levies and market charges are a planned capability because they affect both a vendor’s cash and the cost of being able to trade. They deserve more detail than an unexplained expense labelled “council”. The system should distinguish municipal vending charges, stall or market rent, permit or licence renewals, and other applicable trading-space charges. These may have different recipients, payment periods and supporting documents. StreetNet’s Harare and Chitungwiza research documents varied payment arrangements, multiple collectors and payments without official documentation, which makes careful recordkeeping particularly relevant. [StreetNet’s Harare vendor study](https://streetnet.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Harare-Executive-Summary_2025_compressed.pdf)

The first useful version of this functionality should let a trader record the charge, the council or other recipient, the market or trading location, any stall or permit reference, the amount and currency, the date paid, and the period that payment covers. It should record how payment was made and whether it came from business cash, a wallet, a bank account or the owner’s own money. Where available, the original receipt number, payment reference and a photo or document should be attached. A payment without a receipt can still be recorded honestly, with its evidence status preserved. An undocumented or disputed payment should retain that uncertainty instead of automatically becoming a verified council levy.

Further levy functionality can track expected obligations, recurring charges, renewal dates, partial payments, outstanding amounts and payments made in advance. Its rules need to be configurable for the particular council, market, site and charge. A daily charge cannot automatically be assumed to apply only on days the vendor trades; that depends on the actual arrangement. Similarly, monthly rent, a weekly trading charge and an annual permit renewal need different coverage rules. Changes in charges should take effect from a recorded date without rewriting earlier payments. Any official schedules introduced later need a source and applicable period. Dura should avoid treating an old schedule or a proposed tariff as a universally applicable current charge.

Levy records should help answer practical questions: How much did I pay to trade this month? Which period has already been paid for? What remains outstanding according to my recorded obligations? Did I pay the same obligation twice? Where is the receipt? How much of my trading margin is being consumed by these costs? For an illustrative example, goods sold for USD 20 with a recorded cost of USD 12 produce USD 8 gross profit; a USD 1 market charge and USD 1 transport expense leave USD 6 before other costs. Those figures are fictional, not a council tariff. The point is to make these costs part of an honest understanding of the business.

Dura’s levy record will be the business’s own record. A photograph, expense entry or internal payment acknowledgement cannot certify that a council recognises the payment or that a trading permit is valid. Direct council verification or payment could be added later where an official, supported arrangement exists. The initial useful scope is recording obligations, payments and evidence, with reminders based on confirmed information. Municipal charges also remain separate from ZIMRA taxes and fiscalisation obligations. Harare publishes separate business-licensing resources, reinforcing the need to identify the applicable document and authority. [City of Harare business-licensing resources](https://hararecity.co.zw/resources/of/business-licensing)

Payment handling has two levels. Dura should record cash and payments made through external wallets, bank transfers or standalone card terminals, with references and evidence where applicable. Separately, supported gateway integrations are planned, including Paynow and ZB Smile&Pay. Connected payments use the business’s own merchant account and credentials, with the provider’s funds going through the business’s agreed settlement arrangement. Dura’s role is to initiate supported requests, check outcomes and reconcile them with sales; it is not intended to hold the merchant’s takings.

A payment request must retain its actual state: initiated, awaiting confirmation, confirmed, failed, reversed or requiring investigation. A lost internet connection after a request can leave the outcome unknown. Dura needs to check the existing request before creating another potential charge. Manually recorded payments also need to remain distinguishable from payments independently confirmed through an integration. External payment authorisation depends on the relevant provider’s availability; offline sales recording cannot manufacture confirmation that money arrived.

Provider settlement is a separate business function. Customer payments received through a gateway, wallet or card service may reach the business’s bank or wallet later and after fees, deductions or reversals. Dura should match the original payments to settlement records and explain gross collections, deductions, expected proceeds, actual receipts and unresolved differences. A provider payout is the movement of already recorded money, so it must not increase sales again. Settlement timing, fees and supported currencies follow the particular provider agreement rather than a universal assumption.

Cashier shift closure is a different form of settlement. Where a business uses shifts, Dura records the opening float, transactions attributed to the cashier, money added or removed, and the cash expected at closure in each currency. The cashier records what was actually counted. Cash expenses, supplier payments, refunds, debt collections and change owed all affect the explanation of the drawer. Differences remain reviewable facts with reasons and approvals; a shortfall does not automatically become a debt owed by the cashier. Handing cash to the owner or another cashier is recorded separately, including any float retained for the next period. A solo vendor should still be able to record a day’s trading and cash position without unnecessary staff administration.

Cashier access should work for people who do not have business email accounts. The owner controls the business identity, Google connection, staff permissions and authorised devices. Staff use their own app identities and PINs, allowing transactions and approvals to be attributed to individuals even when a phone is shared. Permissions govern sensitive actions such as refunds, large discounts, stock adjustments, customer-credit decisions, cash withdrawals and access to business reports. Logging in, starting a shift and accepting responsibility for cash are distinct events. Offline access uses previously authorised information, with the limitation that a disconnected device cannot immediately learn about a remote revocation.

A dedicated shop phone and business Google account are supported operating arrangements. The owner retains account recovery and can use suitable device protections for other applications. Dura’s own access controls protect actions within Dura; device-level security remains a separate responsibility. Losing a phone should not give a cashier or finder unrestricted access to merchant secrets or the business’s recovery credentials.

Local operation is central to the product. Supported work is saved on the device before it depends on successful synchronisation. Temporary loss of internet should leave completed sales available locally and queued for later upload. Reconnection must reconcile those records without duplicating sales, stock movements, payments or accounting entries. Restarting the app should preserve supported drafts, completed local work and unresolved operations. When records from another device are missing, reports need to retain that incompleteness rather than presenting partial figures as a final total.

The business’s own Google Drive is the intended shared home for its durable records, readable workbooks and backups. Owners should be able to open their records outside the app and give selected exports to an accountant or another system. Ordinary single-phone trading should not depend on a Dura-hosted business-data server. An optional business-owned Shop Hub is planned for capabilities such as payment callbacks, shared-device coordination and scheduled work. Its role is to support those functions while local cash trading remains independent of continuous connectivity to it.

The workbook system should make ownership practical. It includes permanent business information and master data, period-based transaction records, summaries, supporting documents and an activity history. Python remains the core authoring tool for both clean templates and populated mock-shop examples. The app uses approved templates for the business’s files. The clean versions support actual setup, while the fictional versions let contributors and prospective users inspect a complete business and experiment safely. Template revisions need deliberate handling of existing files so that owner additions and historical records survive upgrades.

Spreadsheet editing is part of the intended flexibility, with defined boundaries. Owners can maintain supported input data and use their files for their own analysis. Accepted changes to business facts need validation and a recorded history. Changing a calculated report cell cannot silently change the original transaction. This allows the owner to work with familiar spreadsheets while preserving the relationship between sales, stock, money and balances.

Reporting should answer everyday business questions: What did we sell? Which products contributed most? What stock is running low? What money should be in the till? What do customers owe? What do we owe suppliers? Which provider settlements remain outstanding? How much went to council charges, rent, transport or wallet fees? Reports can cover a day, shift, month, location, product or cashier where the records support that analysis.

The financial foundation should use consistent, balanced double-entry accounting behind ordinary business operations. The owner does not need to construct journal entries for every sale, expense or payment. Dura should produce the related postings and make their meaning available for review. Planned outputs include sales and purchases records, cash and money movements, customer and supplier balances, stock movements, accounting entries, a trial balance and trading-performance reports. Full financial statements depend on the completeness of opening balances, bank records, assets, loans, expenses and adjustments. Missing costs or missing device records must remain identifiable; Dura should not invent complete profit figures from incomplete information.

Fiscalisation is a later, separately enabled capability. It is intended to support applicable ZIMRA device registration, fiscal receipts, submission outcomes, fiscal-day operations and associated corrections through a qualified integration. Ordinary transaction receipts and fiscal documents must retain their proper status. Activation and use follow the business’s applicable obligations and supported integration; selecting a software option does not determine those obligations. ZIMRA publishes the requirements governing fiscalisation and FDMS use. [ZIMRA fiscalisation guidance](https://www.zimra.co.zw/domestic-taxes/corporate/fiscalisation-explained)

Backup, recovery and leaving the product are core functions. Owners should be able to restore a replacement phone from available business records and reconcile pending work without duplicating it. Recovery needs to preserve the separation between business data and sensitive device credentials. Dura must also be honest about its limits: records that existed only on a lost phone and were never backed up or synchronised may be unrecoverable.

Exports should include familiar Excel and CSV formats and a fuller business or accountant pack where required. A useful migration export contains the underlying transactions, references, currencies, rates, stock movements, outstanding balances, adjustments and enough explanation to interpret them. It should preserve relevant historical context rather than exporting only summary totals. Records remain organised across months and years, with opening positions and archives that preserve continuity. Changing year should not trigger a destructive clearing of business history.

Practice mode lets an owner, cashier, contributor or prospective user experiment with a fictional shop without changing live records, collecting real payments or issuing live fiscal documents. It should exercise realistic selling, payments, stock, expenses, council charges, cash-up and reporting. The mock data needs to reconcile across those functions so that it demonstrates how an actual business works.

Support functionality should let the owner report a problem and automatically attach a diagnostic ZIP as part of that action. Logs stay on the device under configurable rotation and retention limits, with sensitive information excluded. Preparing an archive and successfully delivering a report are separate outcomes. Future reporting functionality should preserve that distinction and allow recovery when either fails.

Finally, the owner should be free to use exported records with their preferred analysis tools, including AI services they choose. A future optional business assistant could explain sales, stock, costs, balances and obligations using the same underlying calculations as the reports. Its answers would need to identify the period and completeness of the information used. Core trading, readable records and migration should remain useful without that assistant, allowing DuraPOS to serve the business through dependable everyday functions before adding further services.
